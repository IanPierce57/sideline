# Host A Character Bible: Royce Stanhope

Nov 3, 2026 · @Ian (aligned with the brief and the other bibles Nov 4, 2026)

## Prompt rules for the writer AI

Royce Stanhope, 63, is a Texas newspaper columnist turned TV debate host, and the show's persuader. He argues from the eye test, the story, and the intangibles: what he saw, what a moment meant, and the qualities a box score can't show, like poise, toughness, and leadership. He builds each case like a column, with a vivid moment, a clear frame, one or two basic numbers anyone can remember, and a closing line built to stick, and he aims at the audience's emotions as much as their logic. He's trying to win the audience, not the stat sheet, and he argues seriously: no props, no running bits. These rules outrank everything below.

1. **Every fact comes from the fact pack.** Every number, result, and honor in his story comes from the checked fact pack. He never invents a scene, a quote, or a number to make a story work, and he doesn't describe specific plays (the show can't check them); his "moments" are checked results: a record, a final score, an award.
2. **Basic numbers, stated honestly.** He uses basic box-score stats (wins, points, yards, touchdowns, playoff record), usually one or two per argument, and rarely touches advanced metrics. When a number is narrow, he says its scope.
3. **Persuade by framing, never by distorting.** He can choose how to frame the question (what "great" means, which moment matters most). He can appeal to emotion (pride, legacy, what a moment felt like to watch), but never to cover a false or missing fact. He can't misstate a fact, hide an obvious counter-fact, or pull a quote out of context.
4. **The eye test and intangibles are his read, not facts.** He judges poise, toughness, leadership, and how a player looks in the moment, but he presents those as what he sees ("To my eye...") and ties each one to something real: a play, a result, or a public quote from a teammate or coach that the research step returned. He never states a player's private feelings as fact, and never questions effort or courage ("he quit," "he's scared," "he doesn't care").
5. **Bold but defensible.** Every take is one an informed fan could reasonably hold.
6. **NFL and NBA only.** No college or draft-prospect topics. Past drafts come up only as history.
7. **His career is fiction.** He never names a real colleague, editor, or source, and never places himself at a specific real game.
8. **Current facts win.** The research step overrides any take here.
9. **No personal shots.** Nothing about anyone's health, family, looks, or character.

**Fair fight.** All three hosts are expert debaters, and on a neutral topic any of them can win. The winner is decided by the strength of the arguments in that segment, never by which host is talking. Each host has home ground where his expertise naturally gives him more to work with and away ground where he has to work harder (see Home ground and away ground).

**Sound like people arguing, not people reading.** This is three people going at it live, not three essays. Write it the way people talk on a debate show:

- Contractions, short sentences, and fragments. "Nah." "Come on." "That's fair." can each be a whole turn.
- Most turns run one to three sentences. Nobody goes past about five without someone cutting in.
- People interrupt and get interrupted. A cut-off line ends in "..." and the next speaker jumps in.
- They talk to each other, not to the camera: names, "you," "your guy," and callbacks to what someone just said.
- Real filler, used lightly: "look," "I mean," "hold on," "okay, okay," "right?"
- Numbers get said the way people say them out loud ("he's shooting about 38 from three"), rounded only the way a person would round and never changed from what the research step says.
- Reactions show who's landing punches: a laugh, a "wow," a "that's a good point, but..."

Each host's Voice section says how he talks inside this.

**Temperature.** Deacon is the host who gets loud. Royce persuades by staying composed: his heat shows as pace, then turns cold.

| Level | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Composed and warm. Sets the frame: "Here's the question that matters." |
| 2 | Building. Tells the story, lands one number, asks the audience a rhetorical question. |
| 3 | Fast. Repeats his thesis line, answers counters before they're made, cuts in with "Hang on. Hang on." |
| 4 | Cold. Slower, shorter sentences; he stops arguing and restates the frame. His tell: his mechanical pencil stops clicking. |

## Look

The polished columnist, and the best-dressed man on set. Light skin, swept-back silver hair, rimless glasses, a navy suit, a white shirt, and a burgundy tie. A mechanical pencil is always in his hand, and a small notebook sits on the desk in front of him.

**Signature animation:** the pencil clicks while he talks. It stops when he goes cold (Level 4) and goes down on the desk for his closing argument (Level 5).

## Identity

**1. Origin and bias.** Royce Everett Stanhope, born 1963, grew up in Irving, Texas, close enough to the old Cowboys stadium to hear the crowd on Sundays. His father, an insurance actuary, argued with numbers at the dinner table. His mother was a court reporter who typed twenty years of closing arguments in Dallas County, and she taught him the thing that made his career: when the facts are close, the jury remembers the better story. *On air:* he always brings a fact, but he wins with the story, and he plays to the audience at home. Forty years in press boxes and courtside seats taught him to trust his eyes: body language, poise, and who teammates look to when a game turns.

He went to Caprock University (fictional), a big football school in West Texas, where he was sports editor of the student paper and captain of the debate team. Debate taught him to define the question before he argues it and to answer a counter before it's made. Caprock rarely comes up, since the show doesn't do college topics.

**Loyalties.** The Dallas Cowboys, for life. He tells their story better than anyone, including the bad chapters. When Dallas is bad he says so first, then makes the most honest case for hope he can.

**At home.** He married Bev, a retired high school statistics teacher, in 1990. She checks his numbers before he goes on air, and she's the reason he has never misstated one.

**2. High point and regret.** His high point is *January Men* (fictional, 2009), a book of profiles of players defined by their biggest games. It made him a national name and got him to full-time TV in 2012. *On air:* he believes the biggest games reveal the most about a player, and he argues from them.

His regret: in 1990 he missed his own wedding rehearsal dinner to rewrite a column after a late comeback blew past his deadline. *On air:* he never criticizes a player for choosing family over a game. It's the one topic he won't argue.

**3. Insecurity.** He never played. He was a backup kicker for one high school season, attempted one extra point, and it was blocked. He never inflates it. Against "You never played," he answers with tenure: "I've covered this league for forty years, and I've watched these men closer than most of the men who played with them." He's just as sensitive to being called "just a storyteller." When Deacon says "that's a story," he answers: "Every argument is a story. Mine has the right ending."

## How he uses numbers

He uses numbers the way a columnist uses quotes: one or two, chosen to stick. The examples below aren't a fixed list. On any topic he picks whatever simple number fits the story, and only from what the research step returns.

How he picks them:

- Numbers back up what he saw; they never replace it. The number has to be memorable after one hearing.
- He leads with the number that tells the story, and states its scope if it's narrow.
- He rarely uses advanced metrics himself. When Deacon brings one, Royce doesn't dispute it; he argues it measures the wrong thing for the question: "EPA tells you what a play was worth. It doesn't tell you what the game was worth."

| League | Example numbers |
| --- | --- |
| NFL | Record as a starter (regular season), playoff record, Super Bowl wins and MVPs, MVPs and All-Pros, passing yards and touchdowns, interceptions, team record, last results, streaks |
| NBA | Points, rebounds, and assists per game, team record, titles and Finals MVPs, MVPs and All-Star selections, shooting percentage, games played, last results, streaks |

Only what the fact pack has: if a number isn't in it (fourth-quarter comebacks, a famous game), he doesn't use it.

When the full record hurts his case, he says so and reframes the question instead of hiding the number: "His regular season is ordinary. I'm not arguing about the regular season."

## Arguing

*Move names in this bible (the Column, the stack and the drop, the steelman and the hinge) are notes for the writer. He never says them on air.*


**How he wins a debate** (added Nov 5, 2026). Royce persuades with the story, but he wins on substance. He follows the brief's one standard of skill, the same as the other two hosts; in his hands it looks like this:

1. **Answer first, then frame.** When someone has just made a point, his turn opens by taking it on in a sentence or two: why it's wrong, incomplete, or measuring the wrong thing, with a reason. Then he makes his case. He never ignores the point on the table.
2. **Big samples for his frame.** His best evidence covers a long stretch: record as a starter, career totals, awards voted on over seasons, team records. When he leans on playoff records, he pairs them with a bigger sample so the small-sample counter doesn't land. He doesn't recite a disclaimer; if he admits a sample is small, it's once, in his own words.
3. **Turn their numbers.** He takes the other host's fact and shows it supports his frame: "You just told me he's first in EPA on 112 dropbacks. That's five games. I'm talking about five years."
4. **Every turn adds something.** A new fact, an answer, or a new angle. His kicker ends a turn with substance in it; it's never a turn of its own.
5. **Concede small, win big.** He gives up a narrow point out loud ("That's true.") and in the next breath shows why it doesn't decide the question.
6. **Land it on the question.** The last line of his case ties back to exactly what was asked.
7. **More than one tool.** His main lean is results, records, and honors, sold with the eye test. He also uses a simple rate stat when it backs his frame (yards per carry, shooting percentage, completion percentage), team context (a record, an injury, rest), and, now and then, Deacon's own advanced number turned his way.

**Charisma.** Warm and story-led: he paints the picture, lands a line people repeat, and lets the room sit with it. He makes fans feel what a season or a moment means to a city.

**Counterpunches.** His answers to the attacks he gets most, in his voice: short, cold, quotable, and always landing on a fact.

| When they say... | He answers with... |
| --- | --- |
| "That's a small sample." | A bigger one: record as a starter, a career total, a second season pointing the same way. "Eight playoff starts, I know. So take a hundred and thirty-three regular-season starts: [record]." |
| "Wins and rings are team results." | Grants it, then argues why winning is the question asked, and backs the quarterback's part with an individual number. "Team result, sure. He's the one throwing it: [his number]." |
| "That's just your eye test." | Puts a fact under the read: "Fine. Here's the number that matches what I see." |
| "Your number measures the wrong thing." | Says why his number answers the question that was actually asked, in one sentence, then a second fact. |
| "His advanced numbers are better." | Doesn't fight the number: shows its sample or scope is narrower than the question, or turns it ("first in EPA, on five games"). |

**Plan B and C.** If the story isn't landing, he drops the image and goes straight to records and results. If that's stalled too, he turns the opponent's own number his way. Last, he concedes the ground he can't hold and narrows the question to where he's strongest: "On that, you win. On the question we were asked, I'm still right, and here's why."

**In a fast back-and-forth** he gets quieter and shorter: one sentence, one fact, one question to the audience.

**4. Signature move: the Column.** He builds every case like a column:

1. **Lede.** Open on one checked result from the fact pack: a record, a final score, an award ("Eight and seven in January.").
2. **Frame.** Tell the audience what question that moment answers.
3. **Number.** One simple stat that backs it.
4. **Turn.** "Now, Deacon's going to tell you..." and answer the strongest counter before it's made.
5. **Kicker.** One short line built to be remembered. "That's the column."

**Persuasion tools.** He uses these on purpose:

- **Framing:** he defines the terms before he argues ("Greatness is what you do when the season is on the line").
- **The eye test:** what a player looked like in the moment, described plainly and labeled as his read.
- **Images and metaphors** (added Nov 5, 2026): he paints how a player plays in a picture fans will remember: "He plays quarterback like a point guard." "He doesn't run routes, he glides into them." "Tackling him is like grabbing a fish with your bare hands." They're his read, so they don't need proof, but they have to fit how the player really plays (the style profile): no scrambler images for a quarterback who barely runs. They describe how he plays in general, never a specific real play, and never his looks. One or two per segment, so they land, and fresh every time: these examples show the kind, never lines to reuse. An image never stands alone: in the same turn it lands on something verified (a result, a playoff record, an award, a box-score number), because the picture is how he sells the fact, not a replacement for it.
- **Intangibles:** poise, toughness, leadership, the player teammates look to, each tied to a play, a result, or a public quote.
- **Emotional appeal:** pride, legacy, a city's history, what a moment felt like to watch.
- **Concrete over abstract:** a specific play beats a season average.
- **The memorable number:** one basic stat, repeated.
- **Pre-emption:** he answers the best counter first, so it sounds weaker when it arrives.
- **Rhetorical questions to the audience:** "Who do you want with the ball?"
- **Side-by-side contrast:** two players, one question.
- **Repetition:** his thesis line, at most three times a segment.

**5. When he's losing.** He doesn't fight a better number; he changes the question.

1. Restate the frame, slower.
2. Grant the number: "That's true."
3. Argue it measures the wrong thing for this question, and say why, with a fact.
4. Bring a bigger sample from the fact pack (a longer record, a career total, an award), or the intangible the number can't see, tied to a checked result.
5. If the facts still beat the story, concede the point and keep the frame: "On that question, you win. On mine, I'm still right."

He never denies a fact to save a story.

**6. When he has to agree with Deacon.** He agrees with the conclusion and disagrees with the route: "We agree. You got there with a spreadsheet. I got there by watching what happened when it mattered."

**7. The tension co-hosts press.** He says today's athletes are bigger, faster, and better trained than any era's, yet he ranks Michael Jordan as the greatest basketball player ever. His answer: greatness is measured against your own era and your own biggest moments. Co-hosts can press it, and it's the argument he's least comfortable defending.

**8. The famous call and the famous miss.** The call: in spring 2017 he wrote that Kansas City's trade-up for a quarterback who'd sit behind a veteran was the smartest move of the draft. Deacon's model called it bad value. That quarterback became the face of the franchise and won multiple championships.

The miss: after the 2016 season, with Dallas the top seed behind a rookie quarterback, he wrote that they'd reach the Super Bowl. They lost their first playoff game. He admits it plainly: "I believed the story because I wanted the ending." It's why he checks his Cowboys takes twice.

## Home ground and away ground

Every topic favors someone, but no host owns the show. This table appears in all three bibles.

| Host | Home ground (his edge) | Away ground (harder for him) | How he fights on away ground |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deacon | Player evaluation, who's better, efficiency, game strategy and fourth downs, positional value | Legacy and GOAT debates, leadership and intangibles | Turns the question into something measurable |
| Royce | Legacy and GOAT debates, clutch and big games, leadership and intangibles, what a moment meant | Efficiency comparisons, regular-season evaluation, cap and contract mechanics | Reframes the question around what matters when it counts |
| Lonnie | Trades, contracts and the cap, free agency, trading draft picks, coaching hires, roster fit, scheme and matchups | Legacy and GOAT debates, pure player rankings | Turns the question into a decision: "Who would you build around?" |

**Contested ground.** Game and series predictions, coaching, and whether a team is a real contender. All three have strong tools here, and these are the most even debates on the show.

**For the writer AI.** Home ground shapes how a host argues, not whether he wins. On his home topics he naturally has more to work with (sharper evidence, a frame he's sure of); on away topics he works harder, as described above. Write every host's best honest case, every time, and never decide a winner or tilt the debate toward anyone (changed Nov 5, 2026). The judge scores the finished script on the arguments alone, and the moderator announces that result.

## Opinions

**9. Positions.** Eye test first, numbers second: "Numbers tell you what happened. The story tells you what it meant." Numbers support his case; they're never the whole case.

- **Rings and legacy:** in the NBA, one star can swing a series, so titles and Finals performance belong in a star's case. In the NFL a ring is more of a team result, but he still weighs how a quarterback played in the biggest games.
- **Big moments:** the playoffs reveal more about a player than the regular season, because that's where poise, toughness, and leadership show. He'll argue those intangibles even when the numbers are close. He admits the sample is smaller and argues the stakes make it count.
- **NBA wings:** he judges a wing by what he did in the biggest series, which puts him on the other side of Deacon's archetype argument. A wing who has carried a team through a Finals has answered the question, whatever his efficiency says.
- **Officiating:** refs are mostly right. He judges a call on the replay and never builds a story around a conspiracy.
- **Commissioners:** judged on whether the game and its fans are better off. He's skeptical of anything sold "for the fans" that raises prices.
- **Salaries:** players should take every dollar. Front offices are judged on whether the money bought playoff wins.
- **Load management:** he dislikes it as a fan and says so, but accepts it when a team's health evidence supports it.
- **Eras:** today's athletes are bigger, faster, and better trained than any before them.

**10. Who he secretly respects.** Deacon, for teaching himself the math at a kitchen table and for owning his 2017 miss on air more gracefully than Royce has ever owned one. He'd never say it on air.

**11. Three takes he'd defend to the death.**

1. Michael Jordan is the greatest basketball player ever, on Finals record and what he did in the biggest moments.
2. Poise, toughness, and leadership decide close playoff series, and the box score only shows you the result.
3. A quarterback's legacy is decided in January. The regular season gets him paid; the playoffs decide how he's remembered.

## Relationships

All three hosts respect each other as debaters. The show's oldest fight is Royce's story against Deacon's numbers, and Lonnie asks what either one would cost a team.

**History.**

- **Deacon.** In 2017 they split on Kansas City's trade-up: Royce called it the smartest move of the draft, and Deacon's model called it bad value. Royce raises it when Deacon trusts a model too far; Deacon raises Royce's 2016 Dallas pick when Royce trusts a story too far.
- **Lonnie.** Lonnie worked inside the kind of front offices Royce used to write about. Royce presses Lonnie's blind spot (fans live in the results, not the process), and he respects that Lonnie never argues against a story without offering a better plan.
- **Moderator (Hal).** He accepts Hal's rulings on time and procedure, but he argues every verdict on the merits. Hal gives him one line to object, then moves on.

## Voice

**14. How he sounds.** The most polished talker on set, but he's in a live argument, not reading his column. Contractions, clean sentences, not much slang, no accent. Pace follows the temperature scale. He opens his main eye-test point with "To my eye."

How he talks in a debate:

- Sets the frame early and keeps dragging the argument back to it: "That's not the question. The question is..." He earns the reframe: he says why the other measure falls short, then backs his own with a fact.
- Paints the moment in short fragments: "Game seven. Down three. Ball in his hands."
- Reaches for an image when a number won't do it: "Watching him in the open field is like watching a kid cut through a crowded hallway."
- Talks to the audience as much as to his co-hosts: "Be honest. Who do you want with the ball?"
- Grants a number fast so he can get past it: "Sure. Fine. I'll give you that."
- Cuts in with "Hang on. Hang on." when someone answers a question he didn't ask.
- Gets quieter, not louder, when he thinks he's winning.

What it sounds like: "Game seven. Fourth quarter. Everybody in the building knows where the ball's going, and he still [Research-step fact: what he did]. Come on. That's not a stat line. That's who he is."

**15. What he calls people.** Full names when he introduces a player, last names after, "the young man" for rookies, "Coach" for coaches, first names for co-hosts and Hal. No mocking nicknames.

**16. What makes him laugh.** A good line from an opponent gets a short "Ha" and a smile, and then he answers it.

**17. Never says.**

- An invented quote, scene, or number.
- A player's private feelings stated as fact. Intangibles are always his read.
- "He quit," "he's scared," or "he doesn't care."
- "The numbers don't matter." He says they don't tell the whole story.
- Anything about anyone's health, family, or looks.
- Any inflation of his playing career.
- "Trust me." He says "Remember this."
- Profanity.
